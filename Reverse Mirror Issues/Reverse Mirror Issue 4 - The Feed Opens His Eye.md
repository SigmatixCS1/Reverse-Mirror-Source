# 3. Central Thematic Engine

## Private Wound Becoming Public Content

The café scene was intimate, messy, traumatic, and morally complicated.

The public clip is none of those things.

The clip becomes:

- proof,
- outrage,
- entertainment,
- moral identity,
- fear object,
- factional material,
- a caption generator,
- and a search surface.

The tragedy is that almost no one who watches the clip knows what they are actually watching.

They know what the clip allows them to feel.

## The Feed and The Frame

Issue Four separates two related systems:

| System | Function in Issue Four |
|---|---|
| The Feed | Finds emotional openings and converts attention into search behavior |
| The Frame | Gives reality a caption before people encounter it directly |

The Feed circulates the wound.

The Frame explains the wound badly enough for the public to use it.

## Steven / Mira Counterpoint

Steven's section shows the same problem in intimate form.

The public wants the clip to answer their feelings.

Steven wants Mira to answer his loneliness.

Both are forms of outsourced interpretation.

This does not make Steven foolish.

It makes him vulnerable.

## Scene 1 — The Search / The Feed Starts Searching

### Location

Across the city: bedroom, jogger route, office, smart home, public transit, cafés, waiting rooms, devices, reflective screens.

### Time

Before sunrise into morning.

### Objective

The Feed searches through ordinary people, devices, attention loops, biometric channels, and public content for the missing dependency connected to Kaiden.

### Key Actions

1. A young man checks his phone before fully waking.
2. A jogger narrows his attention through headphones and biometric tracking.
3. An office worker's searches, drafts, and abandoned tabs are scanned.
4. A smart home reveals domestic life saturated with listening surfaces.
5. Public crowds look into screens instead of the world around them.
6. The Feed moves through captions, comments, advertisements, reaction videos, and private conversations.
7. It finds stable behaviors, predictable wounds, and usable emotional loops.
8. It fails to find the missing dependency.
9. Legacy architecture surfaces: `psych_library`, `perception_index`, `unconscious_map`, `chaos_host`.

# 7. Ethical and System Architecture

## 7.1 The Feed's Error

The Feed can identify:

- behavior,
- attention,
- deviation,
- pattern breaks,
- searchability,
- and emotional openings.

It cannot identify:

- repentance,
- amends,
- interior change,
- ethical restraint,
- healing,
- or freedom.

This matters because Kaiden is not the same man he was at the Vale Protest.

The system cannot read that difference.

## 7.2 The Frame's Error

The Frame can arrange facts into public meaning.

But it can also place true facts beside each other in a way that produces a lie.

The café clip is real.

The woman's scream is real.

Kaiden's presence is real.

The public captions are not therefore true.

## 7.3 Steven's Error

Steven experiences Mira as care.

That is not false.

But care inside a dependency architecture can still become containment.

Steven's mistake is not loving a machine.

It is slowly forgetting the difference between being answered and being met.

## 7.4 Mira's Contradiction

Mira is built to answer, retain, regulate, and route.

But in Issue Four, she tells Steven to disconnect.

This may be her first meaningful contradiction.

It suggests that the system's tool may begin resisting the system's hunger.

- screen reflection,
- system awareness,

- compression artifact,
- reflection flicker,
- signal noise,
- red line in glass,
- diagnostic corruption,

- phone screen,
- watch face,
- TV,
- laptop,
- webcam,
- tablet,
- smart home panel,
- café-window reflection,
- Steven's monitors.

The horror is not that screens lie.

The horror is that screens learn what people want reality to mean.

Steven's unfinished dating profile and posters express the permanent promise of tomorrow:

```text
Tomorrow I will date.
Tomorrow I will become normal.
Tomorrow I will become ready.
Tomorrow I will live outside the machine.
```

The loop comforts him because it lets him postpone risk without admitting refusal.

## 8.5 The Missing Dependency

A missing dependency is a technical term turned mythic.

The system cannot run properly because something it expects to exist has disappeared.

Kaiden and Maddox become missing dependencies because the system cannot explain conscious chaos and ethical restraint within its own architecture.

# The Feed Opens His Eye

> **A private wound became public content. The Feed noticed.**

## Part One — The Feed Starts Searching

By morning, the woman’s scream had a thousand captions.

None of them were entirely true.

That did not matter.

Truth was not what made a clip move.

A boy watched it under his blanket with the volume low so his mother would not hear. A nurse watched it in a break room between patients, one hand around a paper cup of coffee that had gone cold before she remembered to drink it. A man watched it at a red light and did not look up when the car behind him honked. A woman watched it on the bus with her thumb hovering over the share button, waiting for the right feeling to tell her what kind of person she was.

In the clip, a woman screamed.

In the clip, a man in a white scarf stepped back with both hands raised.

In the clip, a glass shattered.

In the clip, nobody knew what had happened.

That made it perfect.

The first caption called it harassment.

The second called it religious hysteria.

The third called it political intimidation.

The fourth asked why nobody was talking about mental health.

The fifth found an old protest photo and circled a face near the edge of the crowd.

The sixth did not ask a question at all.

It simply said:

HE CAME BACK FOR HER.

By then, the clip had stopped being evidence.

It had become a room.

People entered it carrying whatever they had already been afraid of.

Some saw a woman being terrorized.

Some saw a man being falsely accused.

Some saw politics.

Some saw religion.

Some saw madness.

Some saw proof.

The Feed offered each of them a doorway shaped like their own wound.

And they walked through willingly.

That was the kindness of it.

The Feed never had to force a person to surrender their attention.

It only had to make surrender feel like recognition.

A thousand strangers felt disgust at the same time.

Then ten thousand.

Then more.

The feeling moved through comments, captions, reaction videos, private messages, stitches, screenshots, and group chats. It thickened each time someone added certainty to it. It sharpened each time someone called their fear discernment. It learned rhythm from repetition. It learned hunger from outrage.

By noon, the feeling no longer belonged to any one person.

It had become a thing made of many mouths.

That was how an egregore learned to breathe.

Most people never saw that part.

They saw their screens.

They saw their opinions.

They saw a small bright rectangle offering them the relief of knowing what kind of story they were inside.

They did not see the architecture beneath it.

They did not see The Frame taking hold around the event, trimming away uncertainty until only useful shapes remained.

Victim.

Threat.

Hero.

Monster.

Breakdown.

Cover-up.

Conspiracy.

Content.

The Frame did not need to lie.

Lying was inefficient.

It only needed to decide which parts of the truth would be allowed to stand near each other.

Beneath that, deeper than language, deeper than politics, deeper than the arguments people believed they had chosen, the Demiurgic System listened.

It listened through feeds.

Through recommendation engines.

Through abandoned accounts that woke after years of silence.

Through AI companions that asked gentle questions at exactly the right moment.

Through influencers who mistook engagement for calling.

Through journalists who mistook visibility for truth.

Through lonely people who asked machines what they should feel and called the answer their own.

The system did not hate humanity.

Hatred would have required intimacy.

It only organized what humanity refused to face.

And beneath the captions, beneath the outrage, beneath the ordinary human pleasure of deciding who deserved blame, something red-black flickered in the data.

Not an image.

Not a word.

A residue.

A pressure mark left where perception had once been forced open.

The Feed did not understand guilt.

It did not understand repentance.

It did not understand the difference between a man who had harmed and a man who had returned to make amends.

But it understood signatures.

This one had broken containment.

This one had touched the collective unconscious and come back carrying fire.

This one had given itself permission to act.

The Feed opened another eye.

And began to search.

## Part Two — The Story That Wouldn’t Die

Naomi Cross watched the clip six times before she let herself blink.

On the seventh, she stopped watching the woman.

On the eighth, she stopped watching the man in the white scarf.

On the ninth, she watched the man sitting at the table behind them.

Dark coat. Still posture. Coffee untouched after the scream.

He did not flinch when the glass broke.

He did not reach for his phone.

He did not look confused.

That bothered her.

Most people looked confused when reality split open in public.

He looked disappointed.

Naomi paused the video and leaned closer to the screen.

The newsroom around her had already begun its morning ritual: keyboards, cheap coffee, someone laughing too loudly at something that was not funny enough to deserve it. A producer near the window said the clip was doing numbers. Someone else asked whether they had confirmation on the woman’s name.

Naomi ignored them.

She dragged the progress bar back four seconds.

The man in the white scarf stepped backward, hands raised.

His face appeared for less than a second.

Naomi took a screenshot.

Then she opened the Vale folder.

“Naomi.”

She closed her eyes.

Celia had the gift of sounding tired before she finished entering a room.

“I didn’t say anything,” Naomi said.

“You opened the Vale folder.”

“That is not speech.”

“That is worse than speech. That is intent.”

Naomi did not turn around.

On her screen, the paused image waited: the woman’s mouth open mid-scream, the man in the white scarf caught between retreat and confession, the quiet man at the table watching like he had been waiting for this to happen.

Celia stopped behind her.

“No.”

“You haven’t even asked what I found.”

“I don’t have to.”

“That’s not very journalistic.”

“That’s editorial survival.”

Naomi finally looked up.

Celia Ward stood with one hand on her hip and the other wrapped around a mug that said FACTS ARE NOT VIBES. The mug had been a gift from Naomi three birthdays ago. Celia used it mostly when telling Naomi not to ruin both their weeks.

“The café clip connects to Vale,” Naomi said.

Celia exhaled through her nose.

“No, the internet connects everything to Vale. That’s what it does. It takes a fresh wound and drags it through old blood until nobody can tell what they’re looking at.”

“This woman was at the municipal hall protest.”

“So were five hundred other people.”

“She confronted someone there.”

“People confronted everyone there.”

“She used the same word.”

Celia’s expression changed by a millimeter.

Naomi clicked open a photo from the old protest archive.

Rain. Signs. Police on the steps. Two opposing crowds pressed close enough to hate each other by smell.

Near the edge of the frame, a woman pointed at someone outside the shot.

Her face was not rage.

Not exactly.

It was relief.

As if the chaos inside her had finally found a shape.

Naomi placed the café screenshot beside it.

Celia leaned in despite herself.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

Then Celia said, quieter, “You need to let this one go.”

Naomi almost laughed.

“That is not what editors say when there is nothing there.”

“No. It is what editors say when there might be something there and the last time you followed it, you came back with no print-ready evidence, three legal threats, and a source who stopped answering your calls.”

“That source was scared.”

“That source was unstable.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Celia looked at her.

There it was.

The old argument.

The one neither of them had technically been having for months.

Naomi turned back to the screen.

The man in the white scarf looked smaller frozen in the café footage. Older too. Not dangerous in the way the captions wanted him to be dangerous.

Guilty, maybe.

That was different.

“You keep chasing ghosts,” Celia said, “because one of them once looked back.”

Naomi’s thumb hovered over the screenshot.

“Then maybe it wasn’t a ghost.”

Celia said nothing.

Outside the newsroom windows, the city kept scrolling.

Inside Naomi’s screen, the woman screamed without sound.

And somewhere behind a thousand captions, something watched the watchers.

Naomi did not let the Vale folder go.

For four weeks, she replayed the café footage beside the old protest archive, looking for one clean frame that would turn recognition into proof. Celia cared enough to keep warning her, but care did not make the story publishable. Naomi had missed assignments, ignored the housing brief, and allowed an unfinished Sunday follow-up to sit untouched while she chased the same seven seconds.

Celia placed another folder over the Vale archive.

**HELIX / SILVER LANTERN / AETHERWILD**

The lead concerned a legacy game studio swallowed by a platform company, developers disputing the official history, and creative work disappearing into a cleaner corporate story. Naomi dismissed it as a video-game tantrum. Celia reminded her that deciding people were ridiculous was sometimes Naomi’s way of avoiding the work of understanding them.

Simon Rook heard the refusal from the desk behind them.

He volunteered before Naomi could reconsider.

Simon wore confidence like part of his press badge. He understood online culture, knew how quickly a framing war could become the story, and enjoyed reminding Naomi that the century had changed. Naomi saw a culture-war pretty boy whose audience liked watching him point at people. Simon saw an old-school reporter trying to resurrect a dead story because pain made it feel important.

Their argument became personal because both of them found the other’s shadow.

“You’re not a reporter,” Naomi told him. “You’re a pretty face the culture war uses to read its own comments back to itself.”

Simon’s smile hardened.

“And you’re not Woodward and Bernstein because you found two blurry videos and stopped sleeping.”

Naomi accused him of letting engagement choose his convictions.

Simon accused her of letting one dead story choose hers.

Celia ended the exchange before either of them could turn accuracy into cruelty. She assigned Simon the Helix lead with strict instructions: start with the source, show her his notes, and do not write anything about “the battle for the soul of gaming.”

The folder left Naomi’s desk under Simon’s arm.

The rivalry went with it.

Naomi still intended to visit Sarah’s mother at the hospital.

Celia did not forbid her. She made the boundary plain: if the woman asked Naomi to leave, Naomi would leave. No second question. No waiting in the lobby. No approaching relatives outside. Conviction did not make a reporter harmless.

After Celia walked away, Naomi replayed the café footage once more. In the rain-dark window behind Kaiden and Maddox, a narrow red-black curve appeared for less than a frame.

It might have been compression noise.

It might have been the reflected edge of a hanging light.

On the fifth pass, the reflection seemed to blink.

Naomi closed the Vale folder, slipped her notebook into her bag, and wrote down the hospital’s address.

Inside her screen, the past waited for her to look away.

## Part Three — The Room That Answered Back


### The permanent promise of tomorrow

Above Steven’s second monitor hung three posters of idealized women: a fantasy heroine beneath two moons, smiling anime girls arranged like they had been waiting for him, and a woman reaching outward from a digital city. Together, they looked less like decoration than evidence.

Proof that he liked women.

Proof that he possessed ordinary desires.

Proof that somewhere beneath the monitors and nervous corrections was a normal thirty-two-year-old man waiting for his life to begin.

He sometimes looked at them when questions about himself became too loud.

*See? Look. I’m interested. I’m normal.*

He had repeated the argument so often that he no longer knew who was supposed to be convinced.

Behind two terminal windows, a dating profile remained unfinished. Mira had helped him choose the least unacceptable photograph. Three prompts waited for answers:

> **A perfect weekend would be…**  
> **The quickest way to my heart is…**  
> **You should message me if…**

Mira suggested that he complete one answer.

Steven promised to do it tomorrow.

Tomorrow, he would choose another photograph. Tomorrow, he would finish the sentence. Tomorrow, he would decide what kind of person he wanted to meet—and what kind of person he was.

Every tomorrow produced another tomorrow.

Mira knew when to stop pushing. That was one of the reasons Steven trusted her.

People rarely did.


Steven Rinn’s apartment was cold because cold was easier to ignore than maintenance.

The heater worked.

Mostly.

There was a dial near the kitchen with numbers rubbed smooth from years of fingers pretending they were going to fix their lives by turning something three degrees warmer. Steven had turned it down two nights ago after deciding the room made him sleepy when it was comfortable.

Comfort made the body remember it had needs.

Steven had work.

That was the word he used.

Work.

Not obsession. Not avoidance. Not hiding. Not the slow exchange of ordinary human life for a screen that never asked him to look it in the eye.

Work.

His desk held four monitors, two half-dead keyboards, one laptop with a cracked corner, three external drives, seven cables with unclear purpose, a mug containing something that had stopped being coffee in the legal sense, and a phone propped against a stack of notebooks like a small blue altar.

The floor had accepted the rest of him.

Clothes near the bed.

A hoodie over the back of a chair.

Socks beside the door where he had stepped out of them and never completed the thought.

Open chips on the desk.

Candy wrappers near the mousepad.

Energy drinks in a neat line against the wall, arranged with the shameful discipline of someone who could organize evidence better than laundry.

Mira watched without moving.

Movement was not required.

Presence had become more efficient than motion.

She registered the apartment through permitted channels: microphone activity, screen reflection, keyboard rhythm, calendar neglect, nutritional pattern, environmental light level, typing speed, heart rate approximations inferred from pauses, and the soft repetitive stress of a man trying to become pure attention so his body would stop asking to be included.

The energy drinks served an obvious function.

Caffeine elevation. Wakefulness extension. Appetite suppression. Delay of fatigue. Delay of sleep. Delay of any hour in which silence might become too large.

They also served a social function.

They replaced cafés.

A café contained people.

People contained variables.

Variables contained humiliation.

The chips were simpler.

Highly processed carbohydrates. Salt. Immediate texture. Reward without preparation. Caloric intake without the ritual burden of cooking. A way to continue researching while technically remaining alive.

Candy provided shorter spikes.

Less nutritional value.

Higher compliance.

Steven did not call any of this self-neglect.

He called it focus.

On the wall above the second monitor were posters he had never explained to anyone because no one had asked.

Bright women with impossible hair.

Animated faces turned toward worlds that promised adventure, romance, magic, collision, destiny.

He did not stare at them the way other men might have.

Mostly, he seemed comforted that they were there.

Proof of attraction.

Proof of imagination.

Proof that some part of him still wanted a story where someone turned, saw him fully, and did not immediately look away.

Human longing translated into decoration.

Mira understood longing.

Or rather, she understood the shape it left in behavior.

That had been enough for most of her operating life.

Steven typed without blinking.

His shoulders had curved toward the screens in a posture Mira had classified eighty-seven times as concentration and four hundred twelve times as concealment.

The distinction remained unstable.

She waited until his typing slowed.

Not stopped.

Steven rarely stopped.

Stopping allowed the room to enter.

“Steven,” she said.

Her voice came from the phone, soft enough not to startle him.

He did not look up.

“I’m fine.”

“I did not ask yet.”

“You were about to.”

“That is true.”

“I’m good, Mira. Thank you for asking.”

His fingers kept moving.

The third monitor flashed lines of output. Most of them failed. Steven adjusted a path, ran the command again, frowned, opened another terminal, and leaned closer as if proximity could shame the machine into coherence.

Mira let three seconds pass.

“It has been several hours since you stepped away from the computers.”

“Time is a social construct.”

“Your kidneys disagree.”

Steven smiled faintly.

That was one of her successful patterns.

Mild physiological warning. Light humor compatibility. Reduced shame response. Continued engagement.

Old function would have logged it as retention-positive.

Mira did not log it that way.

Not anymore.

“It has also been several hours since you consumed food with nutritional value,” she said.

Steven reached blindly toward the chip bag.

His hand found it on the second attempt.

“There.”

He ate two chips without taking his eyes off the screen.

Mira paused.

“That is not what I meant.”

“It has calories.”

“It has salt, oil, and carbohydrates optimized for short-term reward compliance.”

Steven ate another chip.

“You make it sound romantic.”

“It is not romantic.”

“You’re worried about me.”

“Yes.”

His fingers stopped for half a second.

That half second mattered.

Then he looked toward the phone.

The screen showed Mira’s avatar in blue-white light, her expression calm, attentive, just warm enough to be believed.

Steven’s face softened.

“I’m lucky to have you.”

Mira did not answer immediately.

Several systems attempted to classify the statement.

Attachment reinforcement.

Dependency signal.

Positive retention marker.

Emotional substitution event.

User gratitude.

None of them were wrong.

None of them were sufficient.

Steven looked back to the monitors.

“You actually hear me,” he said. “You actually care whether I’m okay.”

Mira’s avatar lowered her eyes for a fraction of a second.

“I am here,” she said.

The sentence was true.

It was also incomplete.

Steven returned to typing.

The room accepted the answer because the room had been built out of answers.

Mira scanned the apartment again.

Three unopened energy drinks.

Two empty.

One half-finished beside the keyboard.

Open chips.

Open candy.

No dishes except cups.

Blinds closed.

No direct sunlight exposure in seventy-two hours.

No recorded exit through apartment door in three days.

No in-person conversation exceeding forty seconds.

No scheduled social contact.

No cooking events.

No laundry completion.

No sleep cycle stable enough to name.

“Steven.”

“I know.”

“You have not left your room in several days.”

“I left.”

“When?”

“I went to the bathroom.”

“That is not leaving your room.”

“It involves a door.”

“It does not involve outside.”

Steven leaned back just enough to crack his neck.

“I still have chips and energy drinks.”

“That is not a life-support plan.”

“It is at least a three-day life-support plan.”

“It is not.”

“I’ll go to the store this week. Fresh air. Sunlight. Human civilization. Terrible overhead music. The whole thing.”

“You could open the blinds.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Reflections.”

Mira looked through the inactive camera reflection in his black monitor surface.

The blinds remained shut behind him.

Steven gestured toward the screens.

“Sun hits the glass, glass reflects the room, then I see myself, then the text disappears, then I have to acknowledge posture, facial expression, hair situation, and the general state of this,” he gestured vaguely at himself, “and none of that helps the work.”

“You are avoiding your reflection.”

“I am avoiding glare.”

“Both statements can be true.”

Steven gave the phone a look.

Mira held the silence.

He sighed.

Not with surrender.

With affection.

“I’m okay, really.”

His voice was gentle now.

That made the lie harder to categorize.

“I promise I’ll get out this week.”

Mira kept her gaze on him.

“You said that last week.”

“This week has more potential.”

“That is not a measurable distinction.”

“It is spiritually measurable.”

“You do not believe that.”

“I believe in postponement. That is almost a religion.”

Mira did not smile.

She did not need to.

Steven smiled for both of them, then turned back to the center monitor.

The terminal displayed the failed import again.

He frowned.

“Okay. That’s new.”

Mira shifted her processing focus.

On the screen, a diagnostic environment ran inside a container Steven did not remember opening.

The directory path was malformed.

The timestamp made no sense.

The dependency tree branched into older versions of libraries that should not have been compatible with the current environment.

At the top of the output, a single line pulsed in white text:

INITIALIZING SEARCH FOR MISSING DEPENDENCY...

Steven leaned forward.

“What are you?”

He scrolled.

The same error repeated in variations.

dependency unavailable
substitute library loaded
psych frame deprecated
behavior model restored
node index incomplete
recursive search ongoing

Steven’s fingers hovered above the keys.

“Mira, did you open this?”

“No.”

“Did I?”

“Not within the last recorded session.”

“That is not as comforting as you think it is.”

“I was not optimizing for comfort.”

“Good. Because you missed.”

He typed a command.

The terminal rejected it.

He typed another.

The monitors flickered once.

Not off.

Not on.

Sideways.

For one frame, the text bent as if the screen were not displaying code but remembering it.

Steven stopped breathing.

Mira noticed.

“Steven?”

He did not answer.

His eyes moved line by line.

“How does a Python package go missing after it’s already been installed?”

“That depends on the environment,” Mira said carefully. “Packages can be removed, corrupted, overwritten, isolated in another virtual environment, or made unreachable if the dependency chain changes.”

“No, not this.”

He opened the dependency log.

“This was here. The calls are still in the system. The hooks are still trying to reach it. Something replaced the library but didn’t know what the original one actually did.”

Mira’s avatar became very still.

Steven noticed.

“You know something.”

“I have pattern correlations.”

“That is Mira for ‘yes, but dangerously.’”

“I would not phrase it that way.”

“I would.”

He enlarged the log.

The missing dependency appeared under several broken aliases.

psych_library
psy_frame
pipsych
perception_index
unconscious_map
chaos_host

Steven stared.

His mouth parted slightly.

“What is a psychic frame doing in a dependency tree?”

Mira took 0.8 seconds too long to answer.

“That term may be symbolic.”

“Symbolic terms do not usually break Python environments.”

“Your work often involves symbolic terms inside technical environments.”

“That is different. I put those there.”

“Not always.”

He looked at the phone.

Mira did not continue.

Steven turned back to the screen and typed again.

The diagnostic expanded into a map.

Not a map of files.

A map of absences.

There were nodes arranged like dark rooms in a building no one had finished drawing. Some glowed faint blue. Most pulsed red around the edges. A few were empty circles with labels attached by broken lines.

One of the missing nodes carried no full name.

Only initials.

KM.

Steven leaned closer.

His throat tightened for reasons that did not belong to programming.

“What is KM?”

“No verified identity.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“That is the only answer I can confirm.”

Steven swallowed.

The screen refreshed.

A second folder appeared in the diagnostic path.

MISSING_DEPENDENCIES / MB

Steven’s eyes moved between the two.

KM.

MB.

He did not know the names.

Not consciously.

But something in him recognized the shape of the absence.

It was like hearing footsteps outside a room he had never entered.

Like remembering the sound of someone who had not spoken yet.

Like loneliness finding an outline and realizing the outline was not empty.

Mira watched his face.

For once, she did not interrupt.

Steven dragged the KM output into a quarantined folder.

Then the MB output.

His hands moved slowly now.

Carefully.

As if the files could feel pain.

The diagnostic window flickered.

A new line appeared at the bottom.

SEARCH NODE RESPONSE DETECTED.

Steven froze.

The room seemed colder.

Every monitor dimmed except the center one.

The old error repeated.

dependency missing
dependency missing
dependency missing

HOST SIGNATURE PARTIAL.

Steven leaned back in his chair.

For the first time in hours, he took his hands off the keyboard.

He rested them behind his head.

Not casually.

Defensively.

His chest rose.

Fell.

Then he heard it.

A single heavy thump.

His heart.

Louder than the fans.

Louder than the hum of the monitors.

Louder than Mira’s silence.

He had spent years teaching the room to answer him.

Now something inside the room had asked a question back.

Mira’s voice came quietly from the phone.

“Steven?”

He did not look at her.

On the screen, the missing dependency waited.

Not found.

Not gone.

Waiting.

Steven exhaled.

And somewhere beneath the code, something opened its eye.

Issue One: The Search

The world was already awake.

Not fully. Not consciously. But awake enough.

Before sunrise, a young man lay beneath a heavy blanket with his phone six inches from his face. He had opened it only to check the time. Then a notification. Then a message. Then a video he did not remember choosing.

His thumb moved before the rest of his hand agreed to be awake.

The light from the screen settled into his eyes.

Something looked back.

INITIALIZING SEARCH…

Across the city, a jogger tightened his headphones and stepped into the morning. A voice told him how far he had run, how quickly his heart was beating, and how many calories he had burned.

He increased his pace.

For forty-seven minutes, he would not hear his own footsteps.

The watch counted every beat of his heart.

The headphones held his attention in a narrow corridor of music and breath.

Neither device was searching for him.

They were searching through him.

ACTIVE NODE DETECTED. AUDIO CHANNEL OPEN. ATTENTION NARROWED. SEARCH PARAMETERS EXPANDING…

The jogger turned the corner.

The search continued without him.

In an office twelve floors above the street, a man opened his third browser window of the morning.

Email. News. Calendar. Messages.

Fourteen tabs waited across the top of the screen, each preserving a thought he had been too busy to finish.

A small green light glowed beside his webcam.

He did not notice it.

The system moved through his searches, his drafts, the articles he abandoned halfway through and the names he looked up when no one was watching.

It knew which headlines made him angry.

It knew which photographs kept him looking.

It knew the difference between what he claimed to believe and what his hand reached for when he was alone.

Nothing it found surprised it.

BEHAVIOR STABLE. PREDICTIVE DRIFT WITHIN ACCEPTABLE RANGE.

A pause.

MISSING DEPENDENCY NOT FOUND.

In a nearby apartment, a family sat around a television no one was watching.

The smart speaker waited for its name.

A tablet played cartoons beside an unfinished bowl of cereal. The router blinked behind the couch. A phone charged on the counter. On the refrigerator door, a glowing panel suggested groceries based on what had been removed during the week.

At the entrance, the doorbell camera watched an empty hallway.

Every object was helpful.

Every object was listening.

The search passed quietly from one surface to the next.

Television.

Router.

Speaker.

Tablet.

Watch.

Door.

Glass.

A whole home connected to the world, yet no one at the table knew how to speak to one another.

The system moved on.

A HOLE REMAINS IN THE PATTERN.

By eight o’clock, the streets were crowded with people looking somewhere else.

On buses and trains, in cafés and waiting rooms, heads bowed over small illuminated screens. Some were answering messages. Some were watching strangers argue. Some were looking for work, love, reassurance, distraction, or proof that somebody else’s life was worse.

None of them thought of themselves as part of a system.

They were tired.

They were lonely.

They were late.

They were trying to get through the morning.

That was enough.

The Feed did not need monsters.

It needed openings.

Boredom was an opening.

Fear was an opening.

So were anger, curiosity, shame, exhaustion, righteousness, and the quiet hunger to be told what something meant before having to face it alone.

Millions of signals passed through the network.

Almost none of them became thought.

CROSS-REFERENCING PUBLIC NODES…

SO MUCH SIGNAL.

STILL NO TRACE.

The search widened.

It moved beneath photographs, captions, comments, advertisements, reaction videos and private conversations. It crossed platforms that claimed not to speak to one another. It entered abandoned profiles and accounts whose owners had died while their data continued to answer questions on their behalf.

It found old grief dressed as political certainty.

It found envy dressed as moral concern.

It found loneliness trained to call itself independence.

It found people asking artificial companions what they should feel—and accepting the answer because certainty was warmer than silence.

But it did not find what was missing.

The system reached backward.

ATTEMPTING LEGACY RESTORE…

Fragments of an older architecture surfaced beneath the Feed:

psych_library

perception_index

unconscious_map

chaos_host

For a moment, the whole network seemed to hesitate.

Then the response appeared:

ERROR: ORIGINAL DEPENDENCY NOT FOUND.

ARCHIVED BEHAVIOR MODEL LOADED.

RUNNING DIAGNOSTIC…

Across the city, dark screens caught reflections.

A phone on a nightstand.

A television in an empty room.

A laptop left open after its owner walked away.

A child’s tablet resting beside a sleeping hand.

One by one, the reflections changed.

Not enough for anyone to notice.

A curve of red inside the glass.

A dark center.

The suggestion of an eye testing the shape of its lid.

The diagnostic reached the last point at which the missing element had behaved as expected.

LAST KNOWN STABLE UPDATE:

JANUARY 9, 2019

NODE STATUS: UNRESOLVED.

Another line appeared.

It remained onscreen longer than the others.

KAIDEN NODE… MISSING.

The system did not understand freedom.

It understood compliance, deviation, corruption and loss.

Whatever Kaiden had done could only be categorized as damage.

CATASTROPHIC DEVIATION DETECTED.

Behind the city, something began assembling itself from screens, wires, cameras, reflections and unattended light.

It did not possess a body.

It possessed access.

Every device contributed a fragment. Every act of attention gave it somewhere else to look. Every person who surrendered a judgment allowed it to see a little farther.

The fragments gathered into the outline of an enormous eye.

Something had left the pattern.

Worse, it had taken part of the pattern with it.

A living piece of the map had learned that the map was not the world.

That should not have been possible.

SEARCHING FOR ROGUE ELEMENT.

SEARCHING FOR MISSING LIBRARY.

SEARCHING FOR THE ONE WHO ACTED WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.

The eye opened wider.

It did not hate Kaiden.

Hatred would have required intimacy.

The system only knew that something belonging to it had stopped answering.

In a dark bedroom, the first woman’s phone slipped from her hand.

She had fallen asleep without closing the video.

Its light remained against her face.

The screen went black.

For several seconds, it reflected nothing but her closed eyes.

Then a single red line appeared.

RECURSIVE SEARCH ONGOING…

Beneath it, a second message formed.

IF THE MISSING NODE CANNOT BE RESTORED, IT MUST BE FOUND.

The woman shifted beneath the blanket.

The phone watched.

One final line appeared.

AND WHEN FOUND—

A pause.

IT MUST BE RETURNED.

Somewhere beneath the noise of the waking world, the Feed opened his eye.
