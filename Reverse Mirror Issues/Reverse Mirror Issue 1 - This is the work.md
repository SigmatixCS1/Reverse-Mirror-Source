## Scene 1 — Café Confrontation

The café was too normal for what he had come there to do.

That was the first thing that bothered him.

He had expected the place to feel heavier somehow. Dimmer. Charged. Maybe the air should have buzzed when he stepped through the door, or the lights should have flickered, or some old instinct in his body should have warned him that the past was sitting three tables from the window with both hands wrapped around a paper cup.

But the café was just a café.

![[ChatGPT Image May 27, 2026, 04_06_51 PM 1.png]]

A bell gave a tired little jingle above the door. Steam hissed behind the counter. Someone laughed too loudly at a corner table. A student in a green hoodie was hunched over a laptop with three empty sugar packets lined up beside the keyboard. Two older men were arguing about a local construction project like it was a matter of national survival. Near the register, a child in a puffy coat pressed both hands against the pastry case and fogged the glass with his breath.

Nothing sacred. Nothing cinematic.

Just cheap wood tables, warm yellow bulbs, mismatched chairs, chalkboard menus, oat milk upcharges, and the smell of burnt espresso.

Maddox chose a table without asking.

Of course he did.

Maddox moved through the room with that calm, irritating certainty that made everything around him seem more nervous by comparison. He sat with his back to the wall, set his staff against the side of the table, and placed both hands around his coffee as if this were a quiet stop between errands.

Kaiden remained standing for one second too long.

“You’re blocking the aisle,” Maddox said.

He sat.

![[ChatGPT Image May 27, 2026, 08_22_10 PM.png]]

The chair legs scraped the floor louder than they needed to.

Across from him, Maddox closed his eyes and took a slow sip of coffee.

Not meditation. Not prayer. Nothing theatrical. Just coffee.

That almost made it worse.

Kaiden glanced toward the counter, then toward the window, then toward the exit. He had already checked the exits when they came in. Old habits. Bad ones. The kind that used to make him feel prepared, then powerful, then justified.

Now they just made him tired.

“You said she comes here?” he asked.

Maddox did not open his eyes.

“I said she might.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

Kaiden stared at him.

Maddox took another sip.

![[Kaiden Cafe.png]]

He wanted to say something sharp. Something like, _You know, for a guide, you are deeply committed to being unhelpful._ But the words got stuck behind his teeth. They always did now when the old anger rose too quickly. He had learned to notice that. Not stop it. Not yet. Just notice.

That was apparently progress.

He rubbed his thumb against the side of his cup. He had ordered tea and had not touched it. The paper sleeve was softening under his grip.

“What if she doesn’t want to hear it?” he asked.

“Then she doesn’t.”

“What if she tells me to go to hell?”

“Then listen carefully.”

He looked up.

Maddox opened one eye, just enough to let the lesson through.

“She might be giving you directions.”

Then he closed it again.

Kaiden exhaled through his nose and looked away.

Outside, the late afternoon was turning the windows gold. Cars moved slowly through rain-slick streets. The world beyond the glass looked soft and smeared, like someone had tried to erase it and changed their mind halfway through.

He almost missed her when she came in.

![[Sarah's Mom.png]]

Not because she was hard to recognize.

Because some part of him had expected memory to preserve people unfairly. Frozen in the shape they had when you hurt them. Sharp, vivid, untouched by all the time you spent trying to become someone else.

But she had aged.

Not dramatically. Not in a way anyone else would have noticed immediately. She was still young enough that a stranger might have looked at her and seen nothing except exhaustion. But he saw the difference because guilt made historians out of cowards.

There were white strands in her hair now. Thin ones, threaded near her temples and pulled back carelessly with the rest. Her coat hung off her shoulders like she had lost weight in the wrong way. She moved with the fragile caution of someone who had learned to count rooms before entering them.

Door. Counter. Window. Bathroom. Back exit. Corners. Men sitting alone.

Her eyes paused on his table for half a second.

Not recognition.

Assessment.

Then she looked away.

His stomach dropped anyway.

![[ChatGPT Image May 27, 2026, 04_37_48 PM.png]]

She ordered coffee in a voice too quiet to carry. The barista asked her to repeat herself. She did, softer the second time, then seemed embarrassed by having taken up space. While she waited, she kept one hand near the small cross at her throat, rubbing the edge of it with her thumb. Not piously. Nervously.

He remembered that cross.

He wished he didn’t.

She took her cup with both hands and moved to a small table by the window. Same as before, he thought. Back to the wall. View of the entrance. No one behind her.

“She remembers rooms,” he said before he meant to.

Maddox opened his eyes.

Kaiden swallowed.

“That’s new.”

“No,” Maddox said. “That’s old. You’re only seeing it now.”

The words landed without force. That was the thing about Maddox. He never stabbed when a placed stone would do. He just set the truth down and allowed it to become heavy.

The woman sat at the window table and blew across the lid of her cup. Her hands shook.

![[2026-05-28_12-39.png]]

Not much. Enough.

Kaiden looked away so fast it felt like flinching.

For a moment, the café noise swelled around him. Spoons clinking. Milk steaming. A chair dragging. Someone asking for the Wi-Fi password. The ordinary world insisting on itself.

He hated it for that.

There should have been a ritual. Some formal mechanism for approaching the people you had harmed. A bell. A warning. A script written by someone wiser than pain. Instead, there was a woman at a table with trembling hands and a coffee she had probably bought because it gave her somewhere to sit without being asked questions.

He pressed his palms together under the table.

His fingers wanted to curl.

Not into fists. Not exactly.

Just into the old shape.

The shape of control.

Maddox’s voice came low.

![[ChatGPT Image May 27, 2026, 08_21_51 PM.png]]

“Breathe.”

“I am breathing.”

“No. You are preparing.”

Kaiden looked at him.

Maddox’s eyes were closed again.

“I hate when you do that.”

“You hate when people notice.”

That one got through.

The reflection on the surface was dim and warped. His glasses caught the overhead light. For one second, the face in the cup looked older than him, meaner than him, more patient.

He pushed it away.

“I don’t know what to say.”

“Good.”

“How is that good?”

“Because the thing you rehearsed was probably for you.”

Kaiden almost laughed. Not because it was funny.

Because it was accurate, and accuracy had become one of his least favorite forms of violence.

He had rehearsed. Of course he had. In the shower. In the mirror. In bed at three in the morning. He had tried versions that sounded humble, versions that sounded responsible, versions that carefully explained what he had been going through back then without technically making excuses.

He had deleted all of them and kept rehearsing anyway.

He looked over again.

The woman was staring out the window, but not at anything. Her coffee sat untouched between her hands. The cross at her throat had slipped loose from her coat. It caught the light when she breathed.

His chest tightened.

The memory came not as a flashback, but as a taste.

Copper. Heat. A crowd shouting under cold daylight. Signs lifted like accusations. Phones raised. A woman’s voice cutting through the noise, calling him one of _them_. One of Vale’s dogs. One of the rotten ones. One of the people dragging the country into darkness while smiling about freedom.

He remembered her face then.

Young. Bright with certainty. Afraid underneath it, though he had not cared enough to name it fear. At the time he had called it weakness. Contradiction. Leverage.

She had hated Chancellor Vale with the kind of moral clarity people mistook for oxygen. In her mind, hating Vale proved something about her. It meant she was awake. Decent. Safe. Not like the cruel people. Not like the hateful people. Not like him.

And he had supported Vale.

Not cleanly. Not innocently. Not without his own blindness.

But that was not the worst part.

The worst part was that he had enjoyed what happened after she found out.

He had enjoyed watching certainty falter.

He had enjoyed the little crack in her voice when he stopped arguing and started reading her.

Not with magic at first.

That would have been easier to forgive.

At first it was just observation. Pattern recognition. Language, posture, rhythm, the hunger underneath her insults. She had wanted belonging so badly that every accusation came wrapped in a plea. _Tell me I am good. Tell me I am safe. Tell me the people I hate deserve it, so I don’t have to look at myself._

He had seen it.

Then he had used it.

A spoon clattered in the present.

He blinked.

The café returned.

Maddox was watching him now.

Not judging. Worse.

Waiting.

Kaiden stood too quickly.

The chair barked against the floor.

A woman at the next table glanced up, then went back to her phone.

His throat was dry.

“I’m going,” he said.

Maddox said nothing.

He took one step toward the window table, then stopped.

“Do I really have to do this?”

His voice came out smaller than he intended.

Maddox did not answer immediately. He lifted his coffee, took a sip, set it down with absurd care, and motioned with two fingers.

Go.

Kaiden hated him a little for that.

Then he walked.

Every step felt too loud. The café seemed to rearrange itself around him, not supernaturally, not yet, but socially. A path opening between tables. A few people looking up because he was moving with the stiff purpose of someone trying not to run.

The woman did not notice him until his shadow crossed the edge of her table.

She looked up.

Her eyes met his.

For one second, there was nothing.

No fear. No recognition. Just a tired woman in a café looking at a man who had interrupted her attempt to be left alone.

That almost saved him.

Then recognition moved through her face like a hand closing around a throat.

The color drained from her.

Her fingers tightened around the cup until the lid buckled.

He lifted both hands slightly, palms open.

“Hi,” he said.

It was a stupid word. Too small for the distance between them.

Her lips parted.

No sound came out.

“I’m not here to—” He stopped. Too fast. Too defensive. He swallowed and tried again. “I know you probably don’t want to see me.”

Her eyes dropped to his hands.

Then to his face.

Then to his eyes.

There was no red in them. Not yet. He knew there wasn’t. He could feel himself holding steady, forcing his breathing down, keeping the old current buried beneath his ribs where it belonged.

But her body did not believe him.

The cup slipped from her hand and hit the table sideways. Coffee spilled across the surface in a spreading brown sheet.

A few people looked over.

Kaiden flinched toward the napkin dispenser, then stopped himself.

Too sudden.

Too close.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I didn’t mean to scare you.”

Her chair scraped back an inch.

He lowered his voice.

“I just wanted to talk. That’s all.”

Her eyes sharpened with a terror so old it had become anger.

“No.”

The word was barely audible.

He nodded once. Too quickly.

“Okay. That’s okay. I can leave. I just—”

“No.”

This time it was louder.

The café softened around the word. Not silent, exactly. But attentive.

The barista looked up from the espresso machine.

The student in the green hoodie stopped typing.

The woman’s breathing changed. Shallow. Fast. Her gaze moved over him like she was assembling a nightmare from familiar pieces: the glasses, the scarf, the face, the voice, the memory of him walking toward her years ago with a smile that had not belonged to a human being anymore.

“You,” she whispered.

He tried to hold her gaze and failed.

“Yes.”

The confirmation broke something.

She stood so fast her chair tipped backward and slammed against the floor.

Half the café turned.

“You’re one of them.”

Her voice cracked on _them_.

Kaiden took one step back.

“I’m not here to hurt you.”

“You’re one of those evil ones.”

The word hit him in the chest.

A table near the counter went quiet.

Maddox did not move.

The woman’s face twisted, not into hatred exactly, but into recognition poisoned by panic.

“You’re evil.”

“Please,” he said, and immediately hated the helplessness in his voice. “It was never—”

“EVIL!”

The scream tore through the café.

A child began crying near the pastry case.

The barista froze with a pitcher of milk in her hand. Someone muttered, “What the hell?” A man near the door half-stood, then thought better of it. Two women at a corner table looked from the woman to him, trying to calculate which one of them was dangerous.

Kaiden raised his hands higher.

Open. Empty.

But his body remembered other rooms. Other confrontations. His pulse kicked. Heat moved behind his eyes.

Not magic.

Not yet.

Just shame looking for a weapon.

“I’m not,” he said.

Weak. Stupid. Defensive.

Her mouth stretched into something between a sneer and a sob.

“You came back.”

“No.”

“You came back for me.”

“No, I came to—”

“To take me to hell?”

The café seemed to dip.

Not visibly. Not enough for anyone else to notice.

But he felt it.

The pressure.

The way the air thickened when someone’s fear found an old symbol and crawled inside it.

The woman laughed once. A broken, sharp sound. Then again, higher. It did not sound like amusement. It sounded like her mind trying to outrun itself.

“You came to take me to hell.”

People were standing now. Not all of them. Just enough. A man near the counter put a hand on the back of his chair like he might use it as a shield. The barista whispered something to another employee. Someone’s phone came up, then lowered, then came up again.

Kaiden felt every eye.

That was when he made the first mistake.

“Please,” he said, “you’re making a scene. Everyone is staring at you.”

The sentence was out before he could stop it.

Maddox opened one eye.

Kaiden felt the look from across the room like a hand on the back of his neck.

Too late.

The woman went still.

Not calmer.

Still.

Her pupils widened. Her jaw trembled. The broken laugh vanished.

“Oh,” she said softly. “You want them to look at me.”

His stomach sank.

“No. That’s not what I meant.”

“You want them to see me like this.”

“I’m sorry. I shouldn’t have said that.”

“You want them to think I’m crazy.”

“No.”

But the café was already doing it.

He could feel the room choosing a shape. Not consciously. Never consciously. That was what made it cruel. People were scared, confused, inconvenienced, curious. Their faces tightened into little verdicts. Their whispers braided together.

“Do you know her?”

“Should someone call someone?”

“She’s holding something?”

“She needs help.”

“What did he do?”

The woman heard none of it correctly.

Or maybe she heard all of it too well.

Her eyes moved across the room.

The barista’s concerned face became accusation. The man near the door became threat. The student’s phone became evidence. The crying child became a warning siren. Every glance turned red at the edges.

Kaiden saw it then.

Not with his eyes.

Not exactly.

A psychic imprint rose around her like heat from asphalt. Thin red-black fractures threaded through the air near her shoulders, trembling in patterns he recognized because some of them were his. Not all. He had not created the whole wound. He knew that now. There were older breaks beneath the scar he left. A home where love had been conditional. A childhood full of rooms where adults shouted God’s name and each other’s failures in the same breath. A lifetime of trying to become good enough to be safe.

But he had found the fracture.

He had pressed his thumb into it.

And now, years later, it still opened when he came near.

His breathing hitched.

The woman saw his eyes change.

No one else did.

To everyone else, he was just a man standing with his hands raised, frightened and awkward and guilty.

To her, a red glow kindled behind his glasses.

Small. Brief. Almost nothing.

Enough.

She recoiled and slammed into the table behind her. A glass toppled. Someone gasped.

“There,” she whispered.

He shut his eyes hard.

No. No, no, no.

When he opened them again, the glow was gone.

But she was already moving.

She looked toward a nearby table where a teenager sat frozen over a half-finished milkshake. Before anyone understood what she was doing, the woman grabbed the glass, smashed it against the table edge, and held the jagged half in front of her with both hands.

The café erupted.

“Hey!”

“Put that down!”

“Lady, calm down!”

The teenager stumbled backward, knocking into his chair. The barista shouted for someone to call emergency services. A man near the counter lifted his hands and said, “Okay, okay, nobody move.”

The woman swung the broken glass toward Kaiden.

“Stay away from me.”

He did not move.

Maddox set his coffee down.

Not quickly.

Not dramatically.

But the sound of the cup touching the saucer cut through the room in a way it should not have.

Kaiden looked at him.

Maddox’s eyes were open now.

Both of them.

Maddox did not stand. Not yet. His staff rested beside the table within easy reach. His face was calm, but there was no sleep left in it.

The message was clear.

Do not control her.

Do not dominate her.

Do not make yourself safe by making her small.

The woman’s hand shook so violently that the broken glass flashed under the café lights.

Her eyes darted from person to person.

But she was no longer seeing customers.

Kaiden could feel the vision taking her.

The whole room turning infernal.

The man by the counter became a horned silhouette. The barista’s apron darkened into a clerical robe. The student’s laptop screen glowed like an altar. The phones became little black mirrors. The concerned faces became masks with red eyes.

In her mind, every person in the café had chosen the side she feared most.

The supporters. The cruel ones. The laughing ones. The ones who had come to drag her backward into a world where pain meant punishment and fear meant prophecy.

She was surrounded.

Of course she was.

That was what certainty did when it finally lost discernment.

It built a world where there was nothing left to learn.

Only enemies.

Only demons.

Only evil.

Kaiden stood in front of her with both hands raised, feeling the old power coil beneath his skin, offering him the easiest answer.

One pulse.

One thought.

One command, and she would drop the glass.

He could take the fear out of her knees. Freeze the room. Fold the panic down into silence. Make everyone safe. Make himself look calm. Make the situation controllable.

He could still do that.

That was the problem.

The woman lifted the glass higher.

“EVIIIIL!”

Her scream cracked open the room.

And for the first time since he had walked over, he stopped trying to prove she was wrong.

He looked at her shaking hands.

At the coffee spreading across the table.

At the cross trembling against her chest.

At the terror wearing his face.

Then he lowered his palms just slightly, not enough to startle her, and forced his voice to come out without defense.

“I know that’s what it felt like,” he said.

The woman blinked.

The café held its breath.

His throat tightened.

“I know that’s what I made it feel like.”

## Scene 2: Flashback Scene — The Scar

For a second, the café disappeared.

Not completely.

The lights were still there. The tables. The broken glass in her hand. The smell of coffee and steamed milk and rainwater drying on coats.

But behind all of it, another room opened.

No — not a room.

A street.

Cold daylight. Concrete. Chanting. Phones held high like little black mirrors. Signs swaying above the crowd. People shouting words they had already stopped hearing because the words had become identity by then.

Justice. Freedom. Tyrant. Savior. Traitor. Evil.

She had been younger then.

Not innocent. Not exactly. Innocence was too simple a word for anyone in that crowd. But she had still possessed the kind of certainty that made fear feel clean. She wore it brightly. Her coat buttoned wrong because she had dressed in a hurry. Her cross rested openly against her sweater, not hidden like it was now. Her eyes burned with conviction, but beneath that conviction was something softer, something old and hungry.

A need.

He had seen it before she knew he had.

That was always the first sin.

Not the magic.

The seeing.

The protest had gathered outside the municipal hall after Chancellor Vale’s emergency address. No one agreed on what the address meant. That was the point, maybe. Meaning had become something people arrived with, not something they discovered. Vale’s supporters saw strength. His enemies saw authoritarian rot. The Renewal Bloc called him necessary. The Civic Dawn called him a national infection.

She had belonged to Civic Dawn then.

Not officially, maybe. Not in any way that required forms or dues or meetings. But emotionally. Spiritually. Socially. She belonged the way lonely people belonged to movements when movements gave loneliness a moral costume.

She believed hating Vale meant she was awake.

She believed hating Vale meant she was good.

And when she saw him standing near the edge of the crowd wearing the small black pin of the Renewal Bloc on his jacket, her face changed.

Recognition first.

Then disgust.

Then relief.

That was the part he remembered most clearly.

The relief.

Because now all the chaos inside her had a shape.

“You,” she said, pointing at him like the word itself was an accusation. “You’re with them?”

He looked at her, already tired of the day, already hungry for a fight.

“With who?”

“Don’t do that.” Her voice rose. A few people turned. “Don’t play stupid. You people always do that.”

“You people.”

“Vale’s people.”

He smiled.

It had been small at first.

Reasonable, almost.

“And what are Vale’s people?”

Her friends shifted beside her. One of them touched her sleeve, a quiet warning to let it go. She didn’t. She couldn’t. By then the crowd had given her a stage, and the stage had given her courage.

“You’re the reason people are scared,” she said. “You support evil and then act like it’s politics.”

There it was.

Evil.

He remembered the word entering him like a match dropped into oil.

At another time in his life, maybe he would have argued policy. Maybe he would have asked questions. Maybe he would have laughed and walked away.

But that version of him was not the one standing there.

That version had already burned off.

What remained was sharper. Thinner. Smarter in the worst possible way.

“And you think hating him makes you what?” he asked.

She blinked.

“Excuse me?”

“Good?”

Her mouth tightened.

He stepped closer, still outside the circle of her friends, but close enough that she noticed.

“Is that how it works? You pick the right monster, hate him loudly enough, and suddenly all the broken parts of you become virtue?”

Her face twitched.

He saw it.

A tiny collapse behind the eyes.

Not pain yet. Recognition.

Her friends started talking over him. One called him a creep. Another said they should leave. Someone behind them raised a phone.

But he wasn’t looking at them anymore.

He was looking at her.

And the old machinery in his mind began arranging her.

Her words came from conviction, but her posture came from fear. Her moral outrage was extroverted, social, hungry for witness — a plea to the group: _Tell me I am safe. Tell me I belong. Tell me I chose correctly._ But beneath it, buried deeper, was a private wound: introverted value turned inward like a blade. A secret belief that she was not enough unless something outside her confirmed her goodness.

He did not know her whole life.

That mattered.

He did not know every wound, every room, every childhood disappointment, every night she had waited for someone to choose her and learned instead to become useful, agreeable, righteous, needed.

He did not create the fracture.

But he found it.

And because he was still the kind of man who mistook insight for ownership, he reached for it.

“You don’t hate Vale because of what he is,” he said. “You hate him because he gives you permission to stop looking at yourself.”

Her face went pale.

“Stop.”

He should have.

He knew that now.

That was the first place the future could have split.

He could have stopped.

Instead, he listened to the little dark thing inside him that whispered: _She called you evil. Show her what evil looks like._

So he smiled.

Not warmly.

Not humanly.

The air around his hands darkened.

Only she saw it at first.

The red-black shimmer. The faint circular sigils unfolding beneath his skin. The symbols turning like hooks in water. Everyone else saw a man standing too close in a heated argument. A protest argument. Ugly, but ordinary.

She saw the impossible.

Her pupils widened.

He felt her fear touch the magic, and the magic answered like a living thing.

“You want to be one of the good ones,” he said softly. “That’s what this is.”

Her breathing quickened.

“You don’t know me.”

“No,” he said. “But I know the shape.”

The red sigils widened behind his eyes.

Not bright. Not theatrical. Just enough.

Her cross glinted.

His gaze dropped to it.

And he saw the next door.

Religion. Salvation. The child’s terror of being abandoned by God. The adult’s terror of discovering that belief had not healed the original wound. The desperate hope that if she stood on the correct side of history, heaven might finally recognize her.

He used it.

That was the part he could not soften now.

He used it.

“You’re still waiting for someone to come save you,” he said.

Her lips trembled.

“Shut up.”

“You wear that cross like a receipt.”

“Shut up.”

“Like proof that somebody paid for you.”

One of her friends shoved him then.

Or tried to.

He barely moved.

The magic rose between them, invisible to everyone else, and the friend stumbled backward as if the sidewalk had shifted. The crowd reacted, but not enough. Not yet. People were still deciding what they were seeing.

She had already decided.

In her mind, the world had narrowed to him.

His eyes.

The red inside them.

The smile.

The darkness crawling up around his shoulders.

“You’re sick,” she whispered.

“No,” he said. “I’m just honest in a way you’re not ready for.”

Then he did the thing.

Not a spell like people imagined spells.

No shouted words. No dramatic gesture. No fire.

It was quieter than that.

More intimate.

He took what he had read from her — the hunger for belonging, the private shame, the religious fear, the social moral certainty, the terror of being unloved, the need to be seen as good — and he braided it with his chaos magic.

A psychic implant.

A thought-form shaped like a verdict.

Not merely: _Vale supporters are wrong._

Worse.

_Vale supporters are evil._

Not merely: _You were afraid._

Worse.

_Your fear was prophecy._

Not merely: _He hurt you._

Worse.

_The demon saw the truth of you and marked you because you were righteous._

It was cruel because it confirmed her worldview while poisoning it.

It turned her certainty into a prison.

The implant entered through the exact place where she had already been wounded. That was why it worked. His power did not invent the trauma. It reverberated it. It found the old bell inside her and struck it hard enough that every future sound began to resemble the same note.

Her knees weakened.

For a moment, all the faces in the crowd changed.

Not physically.

Not to them.

But to her.

Every person wearing a Renewal pin acquired red eyes. Every smirk became his smirk. Every disagreement became a threat. Every outstretched hand became a claw. Every argument became damnation wearing a human face.

The world filled with devils because he taught her fear how to see them.

She fell backward and hit the ground.

Someone screamed.

The crowd finally broke.

Hands reached for her. Voices rose. Someone shouted at him. Someone else asked what he had done. A phone shoved toward his face. Police sirens wailed somewhere distant or maybe only in memory.

He stood over her for half a second too long.

Long enough for her to see the signature.

That was what chaos magic left behind when it cut deep enough: not just pain, but a visual memory of its shape.

A red-black circle.

An eye inside a broken ring.

His eyes behind it.

His smile beneath it.

Then he walked away.

At the time, he told himself she would be fine.

People were dramatic online. People were dramatic at protests. People screamed evil all the time. People called each other monsters and fascists and traitors and demons before lunch and went home to microwave dinner after.

He told himself that.

Because if he did not, he would have had to understand what he had done.

But the wound did not close when he left.

It kept speaking.

At first she deleted her posts. Then her replies. Then her accounts.

Not because she no longer believed what she had said.

Because belief had become unbearable.

She started seeing red eyes in profile pictures. In comment sections. In strangers on buses. In the faces of relatives who still supported Vale, including the aunt who had once paid her electric bill, the cousin who watched her daughter after school, the old neighbor who left groceries outside her door when money got tight.

They had loved her imperfectly.

That had not mattered.

The implant did not allow imperfection.

It only allowed categories.

Good.

Evil.

Safe.

Damned.

She attacked first with words. Then accusations. Then prayer. Then panic.

Family members stopped calling. Friends muted her messages. People who had known her for years began speaking to her carefully, as if her mind were a cup filled to the lip.

She saw his red eyes everywhere.

On men in coats.

On anchors on television.

On campaign signs.

On church members who told her to rest.

On her own reflection when she woke from nightmares gasping, one hand clamped around the cross at her throat.

Her daughter noticed first.

Children always noticed the changes adults tried to hide.

“Mom,” the girl said one night, standing in the hallway with her blanket twisted in both hands. “Who are you talking to?”

No one, she had said.

But that was not true.

She was talking to the mark.

The scar.

The red-black circle that appeared behind her eyelids whenever she tried to sleep.

Two weeks in the hospital came after she screamed at a man in a grocery store because his jacket bore the Renewal colors. She had not touched him, but she had cornered him near the freezer aisle with a can of soup in her hand, sobbing that she knew what he was.

The report said psychotic delusion.

Stress-induced episode.

Religious fixation.

Political paranoia.

Acute anxiety.

The words were not entirely wrong.

That was the worst part.

Clinical language had a way of being accurate without being true.

No one wrote down: _A man saw where she was broken and used magic to teach the wound a new religion._

She lost her job while she was inside.

Not officially because of the hospitalization, of course. Employers were smarter than that. There were attendance issues. Performance concerns. Restructuring. Budget cuts. Neutral words. Clean words.

Her apartment went next.

By then her relationship with her daughter had become a hallway full of closed doors. The girl still loved her, but love had begun to carry exhaustion. Every conversation could become a test. Every disagreement could become proof. Every silence could become abandonment.

The woman tried therapy.

Sometimes it helped.

Sometimes she lied because the truth sounded insane even to her.

Sometimes she tried to say his name and could not.

Sometimes she drew the symbol instead.

A red circle.

An eye.

A broken ring.

Her therapist asked what it meant.

She said, “That’s what he left.”

And the therapist, gentle and tired and trained to survive other people’s nightmares without entering them, wrote something down.

The years did what years do.

They did not heal her.

They organized the damage.

She learned which streets to avoid. Which colors made her chest tighten. Which words turned the room hot. Which news alerts not to open. Which relatives not to answer. Which church services to attend from the back row, near the exit, where no one could trap her in a conversation about forgiveness.

She learned to appear functional in small doses.

A grocery run. A therapy appointment. A coffee near the window. Back to the wall. Eyes on the door.

Then, one afternoon, in a café too ordinary for judgment, the man who had marked her crossed the room and said:

_Hi._

And the scar opened like it had been waiting.

## Scene 3 — The Wound Answers Back

The café returned all at once.

The broken glass trembled in her hand.

Coffee spread slowly across the table.

Kaiden stood with both palms lowered, Maddox watching from across the room, the whole café suspended between fear and intervention.

“I know that’s what I made it feel like,” he said.

The woman stared at him.

But now he could see what stood between them.

Not just memory.

Not just guilt.

A living wound with his fingerprints burned into it.

And then he realized something worse.

He had not only seen it.

He had been inside it.

Not by choice. Not consciously. But that did not make it harmless.

The flashback had not simply passed before him like an image on glass. It had pulled him through. For those few terrible seconds, he had been present with her inside the psychic scar — astral, silent, witnessing the wound from within the place he had damaged.

His stomach turned.

No.

He pulled back hard.

The red-black shimmer around his hands vanished before it could fully form. The faint sigils at the edge of his vision collapsed into nothing. He cut the current from his chest, his throat, his eyes, every place it tried to leak out through shame.

But she felt it.

Of course she felt it.

Her whole body had been trained by the scar to notice him.

Her hand tightened around the jagged milkshake glass. Her eyes narrowed, then widened, then narrowed again with terrible recognition.

A laugh slipped out of her.

Small at first.

Broken.

“You did it again.”

He froze.

The café had gone almost completely silent except for the child crying near the pastry case and the low, panicked whisper of the barista behind the counter.

The woman’s mouth pulled into a trembling smile.

“You did it again, didn’t you?”

“No,” he said.

But the word had no weight. Not enough. Not after what she had felt.

He heard Maddox’s voice in memory.

Not from now.

Earlier.

Before they had come inside the café. Before the bell over the door. Before the coffee. Before the woman by the window.

They had stood in the rain outside, under the awning, and Maddox had looked at him with both eyes open.

“No chaos magic,” Maddox had said.

“I know.”

“No reaching. No reading. No correcting. No pressure. No spiritual hands in places they don’t belong.”

“I said I know.”

Maddox had leaned closer.

“No, you understand the rule. That is not the same as knowing the cost.”

Now, standing in the middle of the café, Kaiden understood.

Too late.

The woman’s laugh sharpened.

“You did it again!”

This time she screamed it.

Several people flinched. A chair scraped. Someone near the door muttered, “I’m out,” and backed toward the exit with both hands half-raised, as if the room itself had become unstable.

“No,” Kaiden said again, softer this time. “I didn’t mean to.”

The moment he said it, he hated himself.

I didn’t mean to.

How many terrible things had hidden behind those words?

Her eyes went wild.

“You were in my head.”

He said nothing.

Because the truth was too complicated to save him.

Because yes.

Because no.

Because he had not meant to enter, but he had entered.

Because the wound had opened and pulled him in, but he was still the reason the wound had a door shaped like him.

“You were in my head!” she shrieked.

People started moving then.

Not dramatically. Not like a crowd in a movie.

Messier than that.

The young mother grabbed the crying child from near the pastry case and pulled him hard against her coat. The student in the green hoodie shoved his laptop into his bag without closing it. One of the older men near the corner stood too fast and nearly tipped his table over. A few customers slipped toward the door in a frantic sideways shuffle, trying not to be noticed by the woman with the glass.

The bell above the entrance jingled once.

Then again.

Then again.

The barista had finished her call. Her phone was still in her hand. Her face had gone pale.

“They’re on the way,” she whispered to another employee.

Kaiden heard it anyway.

Police.

Of course.

The word struck the room like a second weapon.

The woman heard it too.

Her head snapped toward the counter.

“No.”

The barista took one step back.

“No, no, no.”

“Ma’am,” said a man in a denim jacket near the register, trying to sound calm and failing. “You need to put that down.”

The woman swung the broken glass toward him.

“Stay away from me!”

The man lifted both hands immediately.

“I’m not coming near you.”

“You’re one of them.”

“No, I’m just trying—”

“Demon!”

The word cracked across the café.

Another man had moved behind her. Not close. Not touching distance. But close enough to try if she lunged. He was large, frightened, and clearly trying to convince himself he was brave. A third person, a younger guy in a work apron from the kitchen, stood halfway out from behind the counter with a broom in his hands like it was a spear.

None of them looked like heroes.

They looked like people in a room where something awful was happening and nobody knew the correct shape of mercy.

“Please,” the barista said. “Everyone just back up.”

But nobody knew who she was talking to.

The woman turned in a frantic circle, broken glass flashing beneath the warm café lights.

To everyone else, she was surrounded by customers, employees, furniture, spilled coffee, and fear.

To her, the room had changed.

Kaiden could see the change through the edge of the scar.

The café lights had become red halos.

The chalkboard menu stretched into black scripture.

The phones in people’s hands became little mirrors with watching eyes.

The man in the denim jacket had horns made of shadow. The barista’s apron darkened into something clerical and stained. The kitchen worker’s broom twisted into a hooked staff. Every face wore the same red glimmer behind the eyes.

Vale’s demons.

That was the phrase burning through her.

Not people.

Not strangers.

Not scared bystanders.

Demons.

All of them wearing ordinary bodies.

All of them pretending concern.

All of them waiting to drag her back into the place he had marked inside her.

Her gaze found Kaiden again.

He felt the old current stir.

It did not rise like anger this time.

That would have been easier to resist.

It rose like responsibility.

That was the dangerous part.

His mind began calculating faster than his conscience could object.

Distance: eight feet.

Glass angle: high, right hand, unstable grip.

Nearest civilian: denim jacket, too close.

Barista: behind counter, phone in hand.

Maddox: seated, staff beside table, not moving yet.

Potential harm: immediate.

Solution: one pulse through the wrist. One sigil behind the eyes. Freeze the motor impulse. Drop the weapon. No lasting damage if done cleanly.

If done cleanly.

His hands lifted.

Not all the way.

Just enough.

Red-black rings flickered around his fingers. Thin symbols turned in the air, fast and hungry, as if they had been waiting for permission.

The woman saw them.

No one else did.

Her mouth opened.

He heard himself whisper, “I have no choice.”

The words frightened him.

So he said them again, louder, as if repetition could make them moral.

“I have no choice.”

His palms began to glow.

Across the room, Maddox moved.

Not with panic.

Not with rage.

With terrifying calm.

One moment Maddox was beside his coffee, one hand resting near the cup.

The next, the staff was in his grip and he was already crossing the room.

Kaiden barely saw the first step.

He saw only the result.

A soft crack of wood against bone.

Not brutal.

Precise.

The enchanted staff struck the woman’s wrist just below the heel of her palm. Her fingers opened by reflex. She screamed and clutched her hand to her chest.

The broken glass fell.

But it did not hit the floor.

Maddox caught it.

He caught it with the end of the staff and his free hand together, guiding the jagged piece out of its fall as if he were redirecting a thrown knife in slow motion. The glass flashed once, then disappeared into his grip, wrapped safely inside the cloth hanging from his belt.

Only then did the room explode.

The man in the denim jacket grabbed the woman’s shoulders. The kitchen worker dropped the broom and helped catch her before she stumbled into the table. A chair toppled. Someone yelled, “Don’t hurt her!” Someone else shouted, “She had glass!” The barista came around the counter and immediately stopped, hands pressed to her mouth.

The woman fought like the floor was fire.

“Get off me!”

“Ma’am, stop!”

“Don’t let them take me!”

“We’re not trying to hurt you!”

“Demons!”

Her voice broke.

“Demons, all of you!”

Kaiden stood frozen with both hands still lifted.

The red sigils spun once more around his fingers.

Then he looked down at them.

Really looked.

The old language.

The old answer.

Control dressed as necessity.

His hands dropped.

The symbols vanished.

His breath came out ragged.

Maddox looked at him from across the room.

For a moment there was no sound between them except the woman screaming, the scraping chairs, the bell above the door still trembling from all the people who had fled.

Maddox’s face had not hardened.

That almost made it worse.

He looked at Kaiden and saw exactly what had happened.

Not just that the magic had almost come out.

That Kaiden had almost believed the lie.

I have no choice.

Maddox’s expression softened, but not with pity.

With grief.

That landed harder.

Outside, sirens approached.

The woman heard them before anyone else did.

Her body went rigid beneath the hands restraining her.

“No,” she whispered.

The word was small now.

Almost childlike.

“No, no, no, no…”

Kaiden took one step forward.

Maddox’s staff shifted a fraction of an inch.

Not a threat.

A boundary.

Kaiden stopped.

The police entered with the bell’s tired little jingle and too much noise.

Two officers first. Then a third behind them. Rain on their shoulders. Hands near their belts. Faces trying to read the room before the room hurt someone.

“Everybody step back.”

“She had glass,” someone said.

“She’s not okay,” the barista said quickly. “She’s scared. She thought he was going to hurt her.”

Kaiden looked at her.

The sentence opened him.

She thought he was going to hurt her.

Not because she was irrational.

Not because she was dramatic.

Because he had.

The officers moved in.

Maddox had already handed the wrapped glass to the barista, who placed it on the counter like it was evidence from another world. The men restraining the woman loosened their grip just enough for the officers to take over.

“Ma’am, we need you to stay still.”

“No.”

“Ma’am.”

“No!”

“We’re going to secure your hands, okay?”

Her eyes found Kaiden one last time.

But she was not seeing him anymore.

Or maybe she was seeing him too completely.

The café fell away from her.

The officers’ uniforms blackened into scale. Their badges became burning eyes. Their hands became claws. The handcuffs uncoiled like iron serpents. The sound of metal ratcheting closed became chains dragged across stone.

One cuff snapped around her wrist.

In her mind, a demon locked a shackle onto her.

The second cuff closed.

In her mind, another chain cinched around her neck.

She screamed.

Not in anger now.

In terror.

“Demons!”

The officers flinched but kept hold of her.

“All of you! All of you are Vale’s demons!”

“Ma’am, breathe.”

“You’re taking me to him!”

“No one’s taking you anywhere except outside.”

“You’re taking me to hell!”

Kaiden’s vision blurred.

She began sobbing then. Wildly. Helplessly. The kind of sobbing that belonged to someone much younger than the body making it.

“Sarah!”

The name ripped out of her.

The room changed again.

Not supernaturally this time.

Emotionally.

Even the people who had been angry fell quiet.

“Sarah!” she screamed. “Sarah, don’t let them get you too!”

One officer looked at the other.

“Does she have a kid?”

“I don’t know.”

“Sarah!”

Her knees weakened, but the officers held her up. Not gently enough. Not cruelly either. Just procedurally. The way systems handled bodies when they did not know what to do with souls.

“Sarah, run! Don’t let them get you too!”

They walked her toward the door.

She twisted once, trying to look back at Kaiden, but the officers kept moving.

The bell jingled above her as they took her out.

The sound was absurd.

Tiny.

Polite.

The door closed behind them.

For several seconds, no one spoke.

The café looked wounded.

A chair lay on its side near the window table. The teenager’s milkshake had spilled across the floor in a pale pink streak. The broom lay abandoned near the counter. Someone’s laptop bag sat open under a table, forgotten in the rush.

Kaiden did not move.

Her coffee was still spreading across the table.

No one had wiped it up.

It had reached the edge and begun dripping onto the floor, one brown drop at a time.

Drip.

Drip.

Drip.

The sound was almost nothing.

That was why it hurt.

Maddox walked back to the table where they had been sitting. His coffee was still there. Cooling. Untouched since the moment he had moved.

Kaiden looked at the door.

Then at the spilled coffee.

Then at his hands.

They looked normal.

That felt obscene.

“I almost did it,” he said.

Maddox said nothing.

“I almost used it on her.”

Still nothing.

Kaiden swallowed, but his throat would not clear.

“I told myself I had no choice.”

Maddox picked up his cup, considered it, then set it back down without drinking.

“You always have a choice.”

“She was going to hurt someone.”

“Yes.”

“I could have stopped her.”

“Yes.”

“I could have done it fast.”

“Yes.”

Kaiden looked at him, anger flickering beneath the horror.

“Then what was I supposed to do?”

Maddox turned his head toward the counter, where the wrapped glass rested beside the register.

“Not that.”

The answer was so simple it almost made him hate him.

Kaiden let out a short, broken laugh.

“This was supposed to be an amends.”

“It was.”

“No.” His voice cracked. “No, it was a disaster.”

Maddox looked at the door where the police had taken her.

Then back at him.

“The wound answered.”

Kaiden had no response to that.

Around them, the café slowly remembered how to move. The barista bent to pick up the fallen chair, then stopped and just stood there. The man in the denim jacket sat down heavily like his legs had finally objected. Someone outside was talking to an officer. A woman near the pastry case whispered into her phone, voice shaking.

The ordinary world was trying to return.

It did not know where to sit.

Kaiden’s eyes burned.

“I made it worse.”

Maddox stepped closer.

This time, when he spoke, his voice was lower.

“No.”

Kaiden looked at him.

“You saw what happened.”

“I saw you almost make it worse.”

That was somehow worse and kinder at the same time.

Kaiden’s face twisted.

“I don’t know how to fix this.”

Maddox reached out and lightly tapped his shoulder.

Not comfort exactly.

Contact.

A reminder that he was still in a body. Still in a room. Still responsible.

“This is the work,” Maddox said.

Kaiden closed his eyes.

Maddox’s voice did not soften, but it deepened.

“Amends are not a spell. They don’t undo consequences.”

The words went through him slowly.

Not like a blade.

Like a nail.

Kaiden opened his eyes and looked again at the coffee dripping from her table.

Drip.

Drip.

Drip.

“How do I make amends to someone who can’t even hear me?” he asked.

Maddox followed his gaze.

“You start by not needing her to.”

Kaiden looked at him.

Maddox nodded toward the door.

“Come on.”

For a second, he could not move.

Then he did.

One step.

Then another.

As he passed the window table, he stopped.

Her cross lay on the floor beneath the chair, the chain snapped, half-hidden in the shadow of the table leg.

He bent down and picked it up.

The metal was warm from her skin.

His hand closed around it carefully.

Not as a trophy.

Not as forgiveness.

As evidence.

As responsibility.

As something that had survived the scene better than either of them.

At the door, Maddox waited.

Kaiden looked back one last time.

The café was mundane again.

Almost.

The lights hummed. The espresso machine hissed. Rain blurred the windows. A barista finally reached for a rag and began wiping the table, but the coffee had already reached the floor.

There would be a stain.

Maybe not forever.

But long enough.

He stepped outside.

The rain was colder than he expected.

## **Post Factum: The Shape of Mercy

*Later, he found himself thinking about the old argument again — the one people used to scream over in feeds, forums, marches, comment threads, and dinner tables until the words stopped meaning anything. Back then, he had believed the answer was obvious: you did not weaken the people sent into danger; you did not replace order with theory; you did not gamble civilization on compassion performed by people who had never faced violence up close. And maybe some of that was still true. But as he remembered Sarah’s mother being led through the café doors, sobbing that demons had come for her child while frightened officers did the only thing they had been trained to do, another truth pressed against him. Maybe the old debate had been poisoned because everyone had been arguing in absolutes. Maybe the question had never been whether society needed protectors or healers. Maybe the question was why it kept sending one without the other. A badge could restrain a body. It could not read a wound. A social worker alone might not have stopped the glass. But someone trained to recognize terror before it became violence — standing beside someone trained to stop violence when it came — might have changed the shape of that room. He hated that the thought made sense. He hated it because the old version of him would have called it weakness. But amends had already taught him the cost of mistaking certainty for wisdom.
